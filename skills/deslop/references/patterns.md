# AI writing tells: pattern catalog

Sourced from Wikipedia's WikiProject AI Cleanup ("Signs of AI Writing") plus corroborating 2026 tracking. Ordered by reliability — check the top of the list first, it catches the most text with the least false-positive risk.

## Tier 1: highest-confidence tells

1. **"Not X, but Y" (the contrast-reframe).** Sets up a contrast to correct a misconception nobody actually had. "This isn't just a bug fix — it's a fundamental rethinking of..." Independently confirmed by multiple sources as the single most reliable single tell. Flag on sight.
2. **Em dash overuse.** Flag when more than ~30% of sentences use one. AI em dashes are typically space-padded (` — `); tighter human usage (`—`, no spaces) is a weaker signal of the same habit. Humans use commas, parens, or colons where AI defaults to the dash.
3. **Formulaic summary/transition phrases.** "In summary," "Overall," "It's worth noting that," "Moreover," "Furthermore," "In addition" — used as paragraph glue rather than because the logic demands them.
4. **Rule-of-three padding.** Triplets used as a rhythm crutch rather than because three items were needed: "fast, reliable, and scalable"; "clear, concise, and actionable."

## Tier 2: common, still reliable

5. **Unearned superlatives / hype inflation.** "game-changing," "revolutionary," "cutting-edge," "unprecedented" applied to routine things.
6. **Hedging stacked on hedging.** "It could be argued that this may potentially..." — qualifying a claim into meaninglessness instead of taking a position.
7. **Rhetorical question openers.** "But what does this really mean?" "So how do we solve this?" — used as a transition device rather than a genuine question.
8. **Symmetrical/parallel sentence padding.** "Not only does X, but it also Y" used reflexively, independent of whether the parallelism earns its place.
9. **Abstract, hypothetical examples instead of concrete ones.** "Imagine a scenario where a user might want to..." rather than a specific, real case.
10. **Flat emotional temperature.** Competent, grammatical, and inert — no stance taken even where the topic calls for one.
11. **Generic, plausible-sounding claims with no specific source, number, or example behind them.**
12. **Formulaic section shape.** Especially in "Challenges" / "Future prospects" / "Key considerations" style sections that read as templated regardless of actual content.
13. **Editorializing praise embedded in description.** "This powerful, versatile tool..." doing marketing work inside what should be neutral description.
14. **Closing-paragraph moralizing.** A final paragraph that reaches for a broader lesson or "ultimately" statement the piece didn't earn.
15. **Bullet-itis.** Converting prose that should flow into a bulleted list for no structural reason — a formatting tell, not just a word-level one.
16. **Bold-headline-itis.** Bolding a phrase at the start of every paragraph as a crutch for scannability instead of because the phrase is genuinely a key term.

## How to use this list

- Don't treat any single hit as proof of AI authorship — humans pick up these habits too, especially the transition words. It's a rewrite trigger, not an accusation.
- Tier 1 items alone are usually enough to justify a rewrite pass. Tier 2 items build a case cumulatively — 3+ tier-2 hits in a short passage is a stronger signal than any one of them alone.
- Cross-reference against `vocabulary-2026.md` for current word-level tells (those date quickly; this list is about structure/rhetoric, which is more stable).
